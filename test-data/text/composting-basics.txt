### Setting Up Your Compost Bin

The first step is setting up your compost bin. You can make a DIY compost bin with simple materials like an old barrel, some wood, or even an old wheelbarrow. If you’re not ready to build your own, most hardware stores sell simple compost bins that are easy to assemble and don’t cost too much. The most important thing is to ensure the bin allows for good airflow and drainage. The material you choose should allow air to flow in and out, and it should have drainage holes at the bottom to prevent water from pooling inside and causing anaerobic conditions. I recommend a bin that's at least 3 feet by 3 feet by 3 feet, as this gives you enough space to layer your materials.

### What to Put in Your Compost Bin

The key to a successful compost is a good balance of green materials (high in nitrogen) and brown materials (high in carbon). Ideally, you want a mix of roughly 30% brown to 70% green. Some examples of green materials include vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds and tea bags, and grass clippings. On the other hand, brown materials include dry leaves, shredded paper, straw, and shredded branches. It’s also a good idea to add some soil or compost to kickstart the process. This will introduce beneficial microorganisms and speed up the decomposition process.

Here’s a more detailed list of what you can put in your compost bin:

#### Greens
- Food scraps: Coffee grounds and tea bags, fruit and vegetable peels, and rinds, eggshells (crushed), stale bread (no crusts), and cooked vegetable scraps.
- Garden waste: Grass clippings, weeds that haven’t gone to seed, and spent flowers.

#### Browns
- Dried leaves, shredded paper, shredded newspaper, and cardboard (but remove any wax coatings or adhesive). 
- Wood chips and sawdust (if they’re not treated).
- Shredded branches and pruned tree branches.
- Straw and hay (make sure they don’t have any chemical residues).
- Dryer lint.

#### What to Avoid
- Meat and bones. These can attract animals and pests and create odors.
- Fats, oils, and dairy products. These can also attract animals and pests and create odors.
- Diseased plants or weeds that have gone to seed. They might spread diseases or pests.
- Charcoal. It doesn’t decompose and can harm your compost.
- Diseased plants or weeds that have gone to seed. They might spread diseases or pests.
- Pet waste. It can carry harmful pathogens.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

I’ve made a few common mistakes along the way, and I hope these will help you avoid them:

1. **Not enough carbon:** One of the most common mistakes people make is not having enough brown material in their compost. This results in a compost that is too wet and smells like sewage. The solution is to add more brown material, like leaves or shredded paper, to dry out the mix.

2. **Too much green material:** Having too much green material can also lead to a compost that’s too wet and smells like sewage. This is because green materials, especially food scraps, can break down quickly and release a lot of nitrogen, which can make the compost too wet. Adding more brown material, like dried leaves or shredded paper, can help balance the mix.

3. **Not enough air circulation:** Another common mistake is not having enough airflow in the compost. This can lead to anaerobic conditions, which can slow down the decomposition process and lead to bad smells. To ensure good airflow, make sure your compost bin has plenty of holes or a ventilation system.

4. **Not turning the compost:** Turning the compost regularly is essential to keep it aerated and to help the materials break down evenly. If you don’t turn the compost, the bottom layer can become anaerobic and stinky, while the top layer can become too dry and not break down enough. Aim to turn your compost once a week or more, depending on the size of your bin and how fast you want it to break down.

5. **Not being patient:** Composting is a slow process, and you need to be patient. It can take anywhere from a few months to a year for the materials to break down completely. Don’t get discouraged if it takes a while; just keep adding materials, turning the compost, and giving it time to work its magic.

### Maintaining Your Compost

Once your compost is up and running, here are a few tips to keep it healthy:

- **Water regularly:** Keep the compost moist, but not too wet. If it’s too dry, it won’t decompose properly, and if it’s too wet, it will smell bad and attract pests.
- **Turn it regularly:** As I mentioned earlier, turning the compost regularly will help it break down evenly and aerate the materials. You can use a compost turning tool or a pitchfork.
- **Monitor the temperature:** The temperature of the compost should be around 130-160°F (54-71°C). If it’s too low, you might not be adding enough brown material or it might not be turning regularly. If it’s too high, it might be too wet or you might be adding too much green material.
- **Add more material as needed:** As your compost breaks down, it will get smaller and lighter. Keep adding more materials as needed to maintain the right balance and volume.

### Harvesting Your Compost

When it’s time to harvest your compost, it’s usually ready when it looks and smells like well-aged soil. It should be dark, crumbly, and have a rich, earthy smell. It’s ready to use when you can’t recognize any of the original materials. If you’re not sure if it’s ready, you can always test it by planting a small seed in it. If the seed germinates, it’s ready to use.
